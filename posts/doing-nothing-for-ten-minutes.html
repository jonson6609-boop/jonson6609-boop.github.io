The case for doing absolutely nothing for ten minutes

Published August 22, 2026 · 4 min read · By Jonah Pierce

Photo via Unsplash

Doing nothing has become oddly hard to do on purpose. Not resting while also scrolling, not relaxing with a podcast running in the background — actually nothing. No phone, no task, no plan for the next ten minutes. It feels unproductive the moment you try it, which is exactly why almost nobody does.

Why "nothing" keeps getting filled in

A quiet ten minutes rarely stays quiet on its own. The phone comes out "just to check one thing," or a to-do list starts assembling itself in the background. None of that is a character flaw — it's just that unstructured time has become genuinely unfamiliar, so the mind reaches for something to do with it almost automatically.

What actually happens if you let it stay empty

The first few minutes of genuine stillness tend to feel restless, even mildly uncomfortable — an itch to check something, fix something, or at least think about something specific. That discomfort usually passes if you sit through it rather than resolving it. What's often on the other side isn't boredom, but a kind of settling that constant low-level stimulation doesn't leave much room for.

This isn't the same as meditation

There's no technique here, no breath count, no app guiding you through it. Just sitting, or standing, or lying somewhere without reaching for anything to fill the time. It can be indoors or outdoors, though a change of scenery — a porch, a window, somewhere outside — tends to make it easier to actually stay in it rather than drift back toward a screen out of habit.

Why this is worth doing even though it produces nothing

Almost everything else in a day is measured by what it produces. Ten minutes of doing nothing produces nothing, deliberately, and that's precisely what makes it different from every other block of time. It's not a technique for getting more done afterward, though that's often a side effect — it's one of the only times attention isn't being asked to do anything at all.

Most of the day asks something of your attention. Ten empty minutes is one of the few times it doesn't have to answer.

Try it once today

Pick ten minutes — waiting for something, right after waking up, before starting dinner — and let it stay genuinely empty. No phone, no plan, nothing to accomplish. It will likely feel unproductive the whole time. That's fine. It's not supposed to feel like something you've achieved.
