What actually helped when I burned out at 31

Published August 4, 2026 · 10 min read · By Jonah Pierce

I want to write this without the version of a burnout story that ends with someone quitting their job, moving to the coast, and starting a candle business. That's not what happened to me, and I think stories like that make recovery feel like it requires circumstances most people don't actually have available. What I did was smaller, slower, and stayed inside the same job and the same life.

What it actually felt like, not just "stress"

By the January I'm describing, I wasn't sleeping through a single night. I'd wake at 3am with my mind already running through the next day's tasks, and I couldn't tell anymore whether I was tired or just permanently keyed up. The specific moment I remember clearly: crying in my car in a grocery store parking lot because I couldn't decide what to make for dinner. Not because dinner mattered — because I had nothing left to make even a small decision with.

I'd been calling it "a busy few months" for the better part of a year at that point.

What I tried first, and why it didn't hold

My first response was more self-care in the conventional sense — a meditation app, an earlier bedtime, a weekend off. All reasonable, none of it touched the actual problem, because the problem wasn't a lack of relaxation techniques. It was that I'd said yes to increasing amounts of work for roughly eighteen months straight, and no amount of evening meditation offsets that math.

The first real change: one meeting, said out loud

In February, I asked my manager for a meeting specifically to talk about workload, not performance. That conversation was the hardest part of the whole nine months, harder than anything that came after. I'd built up a story that asking for less meant admitting I couldn't handle my job, and saying it out loud in a room made that fear concrete in a way it hadn't been just thinking it privately.

The actual outcome was undramatic: two ongoing projects got reassigned to a colleague over the following month. Nobody was upset. The version of that conversation I'd feared for a year turned out to take about twenty minutes.

Months three through six: slower than I expected

I wish I could say the workload change fixed things quickly. It didn't. My sleep stayed broken for another two months even after the actual work stress eased, which was its own kind of frustrating — like my body hadn't gotten the memo that the immediate cause had changed. A doctor I saw in April described this as fairly normal, that the nervous system doesn't necessarily reset the moment the external pressure does, and that framing actually helped more than any specific advice she gave, because it meant I stopped treating the ongoing exhaustion as a sign I was doing recovery wrong.

What did shift things, gradually, was smaller and more boring than I expected: a fixed time I logged off each day, enforced by literally closing my laptop rather than trusting willpower; and telling two close friends explicitly what was going on, which meant I stopped performing "fine" in conversations that used to drain what little energy I had left.

What I got wrong along the way

Around month five, feeling slightly better, I said yes to a new side project, convinced I'd "learned my lesson" and could manage it differently this time. I hadn't, and I couldn't, not yet — within three weeks the same 3am wake-ups were back. I dropped the project in June. That relapse taught me more clearly than the original burnout did that recovery isn't a line you cross once; it's a capacity that comes back slowly, and testing it too early just tells you it isn't back yet.

Nobody warned me that getting better wouldn't feel like relief the whole way through. Some of it just felt like slowly, unevenly, getting my own attention back.

Where I was by September

Sleep came back first, gradually, over about six weeks. The 3am parking-lot version of decision-making came back last, closer to month eight, and I noticed it mostly in retrospect — realizing one week that ordinary small decisions had stopped feeling exhausting, without a specific moment I could point to as "the day it got better."

What I'd say to someone in the version of it I was in

The conversation you're avoiding about workload is probably the actual lever, not the relaxation habit you're adding on top of an unchanged load. And if you do the hard conversation and start recovering, expect it to be slower and less linear than feels fair — testing your capacity too early, the way I did in month five, is common, and it doesn't erase the progress you'd already made, it just means you're not fully there yet.

This is one person's experience, not medical advice. If you're dealing with burnout, exhaustion, or ongoing sleep problems, it's worth talking to a doctor or therapist.
