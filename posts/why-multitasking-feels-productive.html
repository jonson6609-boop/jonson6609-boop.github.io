Why multitasking feels productive even though it isn't

Published August 25, 2026 · 4 min read · By Maya Reyes

Switching between three tasks feels busier and more urgent than sitting with one task for the same amount of time. That feeling is real — it just isn't the same thing as getting more done. Busy and effective are separate qualities, and multitasking is very good at producing one without the other.

What's actually happening when you multitask

Most "multitasking" isn't truly simultaneous — it's rapid switching between tasks, with a small cost each time your attention re-orients to what you just left. Individually, that cost is a few seconds. Repeated dozens of times across an afternoon, it adds up to a meaningful chunk of lost time that never shows up as an obvious event, just a slower overall pace.

Why it still feels productive

Switching tasks creates a sense of motion — you're doing something new every few minutes, which reads as progress even when the underlying output is slower than focusing on one thing would have been. The feeling of busyness and the reality of output are measuring two different things, and multitasking is specifically good at boosting the first while quietly taxing the second.

Where multitasking is actually fine

Not every combination is costly. Pairing a task that needs real attention (writing, problem-solving) with one that doesn't (folding laundry, walking) works fine, because only one of them is actually competing for focus. The costly kind is switching between two tasks that both require real attention — that's where the hidden cost shows up.

A simple test before combining tasks

Ask: does this task need me to actually think, or just to be present? If both tasks need real thought, doing them "at the same time" is really just fast switching, with the cost that comes with it. If only one does, combining them is usually free.

Multitasking doesn't make two hard tasks fit into one slot. It just charges a switching fee every time you move between them.

Try single-tasking the next hard thing

Next time you catch yourself juggling two tasks that both need real focus, pick one and give it your full attention for a set stretch before switching. It'll feel slower in the moment — that's usually the sign it's actually working.
