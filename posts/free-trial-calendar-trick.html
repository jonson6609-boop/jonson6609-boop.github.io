The one calendar trick that stops free trials from costing you

Published August 20, 2026 · 4 min read · By Maya Reyes

Free trials are designed to be forgotten. The sign-up takes thirty seconds and asks for a card; the cancellation, three weeks later, requires you to actually remember it exists on a specific date. Most people don't, and that's exactly the gap the business model relies on.

The fix: set the reminder the moment you sign up, not later

The instant you start a free trial, open your phone's calendar and add a reminder for two days before it converts to paid — not on the exact day, since a last-minute scramble is how these slip through. Title it plainly: "Cancel [service] or keep paying." Two days of buffer gives you room if you forget the first alert.

Why "I'll remember" doesn't work

Three weeks is long enough for a free trial to stop feeling urgent and start feeling like background noise. The sign-up moment is the only time it has your full attention — waiting until later to "remember to cancel" is relying on a version of you with far less context than the version signing up right now.

When the reminder goes off, make an actual decision

Don't just dismiss it — actively choose. If you've used the service and want to keep it, let it convert on purpose. If not, cancel it that day. The point of the reminder isn't automatically cancelling everything, it's making sure the choice is deliberate rather than accidental.

A small trick for trials that make cancelling difficult

Some services bury the cancel button behind several menus specifically to cause fatigue. When you set the calendar reminder, also jot down where the cancel option lives — "Settings → Membership → Cancel," for instance — so future-you doesn't have to hunt for it while already annoyed.

The free trial isn't the trap. Forgetting between the sign-up and the charge is.

Make it a reflex

From now on, treat "start a free trial" and "set a cancellation reminder" as one single action, not two separate steps. It takes fifteen seconds and it's the only real defense against a charge you never meant to keep paying.
