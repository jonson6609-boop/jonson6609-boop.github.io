The five-minute rule for starting a task you're dreading

Published August 23, 2026 · 4 min read · By Maya Reyes

The hardest part of a dreaded task is almost never the task itself — it's the decision to start it. Once you're actually in motion, most tasks turn out to be more tolerable than they looked from the outside. The five-minute rule exists to get past exactly that starting resistance, without asking you to commit to anything bigger.

The rule: commit to five minutes, not the whole task

Set a timer for five minutes and agree to work on the dreaded task for just that long. When it goes off, you're free to stop — no guilt, no "but you should keep going." The only commitment is the five minutes, nothing more.

Why this works when "just start it" doesn't

"Just start it" still asks you to mentally sign up for the entire task, which is exactly what's causing the resistance. Five minutes is small enough to agree to even when the whole task feels overwhelming — it sidesteps the size of the task rather than trying to argue you out of dreading it.

What usually happens when the timer goes off

Most of the time, momentum takes over and continuing feels easier than stopping did five minutes earlier — the initial resistance was the barrier, not the task itself. Some of the time, you genuinely stop at five minutes, and that's fine too; you've still made real progress on something that was otherwise sitting untouched.

Why "no guilt if you stop" matters

If the rule secretly expects you to keep going, it stops being five minutes and becomes the same overwhelming task with an extra step. The permission to genuinely stop is what makes it easy to start again next time — a rule that quietly demands more than it promises gets avoided the same way the original task did.

You're not tricking yourself into doing more. You're removing the one barrier — starting — that was blocking everything else.

Use it on whatever you're avoiding right now

Think of the task you've been putting off longest. Set a timer for five minutes and start it now, with full permission to stop when the timer ends. More often than not, five minutes turns into something longer — but even if it doesn't, it's five minutes more than the task had before.
