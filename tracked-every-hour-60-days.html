I tracked every hour for 60 days. Here's what actually wasted my time

Published August 5, 2026 · 10 min read · By Maya Reyes

I expected, going into this, that the answer would be social media. Everyone's answer is supposed to be social media. I tracked every hour of my waking life for sixty days, in fifteen-minute blocks, in a plain notes app, and social media came in fourth. I want to walk through what I actually found, because the real answer surprised me enough that I think it's worth more than another "delete your apps" post.

How the tracking actually worked

Every time I switched tasks, I logged what I'd just finished and roughly how long it took, using broad categories: deep work, admin, meetings, breaks, phone, other. No app, no automation — just a running note on my phone, updated maybe fifteen to twenty times a day. It was tedious for the first week, and then it became close to automatic, which is when the data actually started meaning something.

What I expected to find

I assumed phone and social media use would dominate. I checked my phone's built-in screen time report going into this, and it said 2 hours 40 minutes a day — already more than I wanted to admit to, and I braced for the hourly log to make it look even worse.

What I actually found

Phone and social media averaged out to 1 hour 55 minutes a day across the sixty days — lower than the screen time report, and lower than I expected, though still not nothing. It was real, but it wasn't the biggest category, and it wasn't close.

The actual biggest time sink, at just over 3 hours a day on average, was something I'd been mentally filing under "work" without examining closely: task-switching overhead. Not any single meeting or any single distraction, but the accumulated minutes of re-orienting myself every time I moved between an email, a Slack message, a document, and back to whatever I'd actually been trying to focus on. Individually these transitions were two or three minutes each. Logged sixty times a day, they added up to more lost time than anything else I tracked.

The specific pattern that gave it away

Around day twenty, I noticed a repeating entry: "email (6 min), back to report (4 min), Slack (3 min), back to report (2 min)." That fifteen-minute stretch produced about nine minutes of actual report-writing and six minutes of context-switching that felt, in the moment, like normal working. It was the accumulation of stretches exactly like that one, dozens of times a day, that built the 3-hour number — not one obvious villain, but a pattern I'd never have spotted without writing every switch down.

What changed once I could see it

I started closing email and Slack during anything I'd logged as "deep work," checking both only at fixed points — once mid-morning, once after lunch. Over the following three weeks, the task-switching category dropped from roughly 3 hours to just under 1 hour 40 minutes a day, and deep work time grew by almost exactly that difference, which felt less like adding willpower and more like removing a leak.

What didn't change, and why I stopped trying to force it

Phone use stayed almost exactly where it started, around 1 hour 50 minutes, even after I tried cutting it deliberately for about ten days in the second half of the tracking period. I eventually decided that number wasn't actually the problem worth solving for me — it was already lower than I'd feared, and the real gain had come from somewhere else entirely.

The category I was most worried about turned out to be roughly fine. The one I wasn't tracking mentally at all turned out to be the real leak.

Why I'd suggest actually logging, not guessing

If I'd guessed where my time went instead of logging it, I'd have "fixed" the wrong thing — cut my phone use further, felt slightly virtuous, and left the actual 3-hour leak completely untouched. The specific, slightly tedious act of writing down every switch for two months is what surfaced a pattern that felt invisible while it was happening. I don't think I'd have believed the task-switching number without the log in front of me.

Sixty days is a lot. Two weeks would likely show the same pattern, if writing every switch down for two months sounds like more than you want to take on.
